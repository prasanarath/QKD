What is Quantum Cryptography?

Quantum cryptography is a method of secure communication that uses quantum mechanical properties to ensure the security of cryptographic keys. Unlike classical cryptography, which relies on mathematical complexity, quantum cryptography is based on the fundamental laws of physics.

Classical vs. Quantum Cryptography

🔒 Classical Cryptography

  • Based on mathematical complexity
  • Security depends on computational difficulty
  • Vulnerable to quantum computers
  • Examples: RSA, ECC, AES

⚛️ Quantum Cryptography

  • Based on physical laws of nature
  • Security guaranteed by quantum mechanics
  • Unconditionally secure against any attack
  • Examples: BB84, E91, BBM92

Why Security Using Quantum Principles?

Quantum mechanics provides several key advantages:

  • No-Cloning Theorem: Quantum states cannot be perfectly copied
  • Measurement Disturbance: Measuring a quantum state changes it
  • Uncertainty Principle: Certain properties cannot be simultaneously known
  • Entanglement: Particles can be correlated in ways impossible classically

Historical Background

The BB84 protocol was developed by Charles Bennett and Gilles Brassard in 1984, marking the birth of quantum key distribution (QKD). It was the first practical protocol to demonstrate the potential of quantum mechanics for secure communication.

Core Concept

BB84 uses the polarization of individual photons to encode binary information (0s and 1s). The security comes from the fact that measuring a photon's polarization in the wrong basis gives random results.

Two Measurement Bases

➕ Rectilinear Basis (+)

Measures horizontal (→) and vertical (↑) polarization

→ ↑

❌ Diagonal Basis (×)

Measures diagonal (↗) and anti-diagonal (↘) polarization

↗ ↘

Key Properties

  • Uses single photons (quantum particles of light)
  • Impossible to clone quantum states perfectly
  • Measurement disturbs the quantum state
  • Provides information-theoretic security

Key Security Features

🚫 No-Cloning Theorem

It's impossible to create a perfect copy of an unknown quantum state. This means Eve cannot intercept and copy the photons without detection.

⚠️ Measurement Disturbance

When Eve measures a photon in the wrong basis, she disturbs the quantum state. This disturbance can be detected by Alice and Bob.

🔍 Uncertainty Principle

Eve cannot simultaneously know both the bit value and the basis used, making it impossible to extract information without introducing errors.

Eavesdropper Detection

If Eve tries to intercept the photons:

  • She must measure each photon to read the information
  • If she uses the wrong basis, she gets random results
  • Her measurement disturbs the photon's state
  • This disturbance increases the error rate between Alice and Bob
  • Higher error rates indicate potential eavesdropping

Bob's Measurement Process

Bob receives the photons and must measure each one to determine the encoded bit. Like Alice, he randomly chooses a measurement basis for each photon.

1️⃣ Receive Photon

Bob receives a photon from the quantum channel

2️⃣ Choose Basis

Randomly select + or × basis for measurement

3️⃣ Measure & Record

Record the measurement result (0 or 1)

Measurement Outcomes

✅ Basis Match

When Bob's basis matches Alice's basis, he gets the correct bit with 100% accuracy.

Example: Alice sends 1 in + basis (↑), Bob measures in + basis → Result: 1 ✓

❌ Basis Mismatch

When Bob's basis differs from Alice's, he gets a random result (50% chance of 0 or 1).

Example: Alice sends 1 in + basis (↑), Bob measures in × basis → Random result: 0 or 1

Public Basis Exchange

After Bob has measured all photons, Alice and Bob communicate over a public (but authenticated) classical channel to compare their basis choices. This step is crucial for key generation.

1️⃣ Public Communication

Key Insights

  • Public Information: Basis choices are not secret and can be shared publicly
  • Private Information: The actual bit values remain secret
  • Key Length: Raw key is typically 25-50% of transmitted bits
  • Security: Eve learns nothing useful from basis information alone

Detecting Eavesdropping

One of the most powerful features of BB84 is its ability to detect eavesdropping attempts. This is achieved through error rate analysis and privacy amplification.

🔍 Error Rate Analysis

Alice and Bob compare a subset of their raw key to estimate the error rate. In the absence of eavesdropping, the error rate should be very low (typically < 2%).

📊 Privacy Amplification

If the error rate is acceptable, they apply privacy amplification techniques to distill a shorter, more secure final key.

How Eve's Interference Creates Errors

🚫 Without Eve (Normal Operation)

  • Alice sends photon in + basis
  • Bob measures in + basis
  • Result: Perfect correlation
  • Error rate: ~0%

🕵️ With Eve (Eavesdropping)

  • Alice sends photon in + basis
  • Eve intercepts and measures in × basis
  • Eve's measurement disturbs the state
  • Bob measures disturbed photon
  • Result: Increased error rate (~25%)

Key Distillation Process

After successful eavesdropper detection, Alice and Bob perform key distillation to convert their raw key into a final, highly secure secret key suitable for cryptographic applications.

1️⃣ Error Correction

Correct any remaining bit errors in the raw key using error correction codes

2️⃣ Privacy Amplification

Apply cryptographic hash functions to eliminate any information Eve might have gained

3️⃣ Final Key Generation

Generate the final secret key with proven security guarantees

Final Key Properties

🔒 Information-Theoretic Security

The final key is secure against any computational attack, regardless of Eve's computing power

📏 Optimal Length

The key length is optimized to maximize security while maintaining efficiency

🎲 Perfect Randomness

Each bit is truly random and unpredictable to any adversary

Applications of the Final Key

🔐 One-Time Pad

Use the key for unbreakable encryption by XORing with plaintext

🔑 Symmetric Encryption

Use as a key for AES or other symmetric encryption algorithms

🔐 Authentication

Use for message authentication codes (MACs) or digital signatures

Security Guarantees

  • Unconditional Security: Secure against any future computational attack
  • Forward Secrecy: Compromised keys don't affect past communications
  • Eavesdropper Detection: Any interception attempt is detected with high probability
  • Information-Theoretic: Security based on laws of physics, not computational assumptions
